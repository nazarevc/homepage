Career and research. Updated Nov 28, 2026

Steven Waterhouse

Steven Waterhouse is a machine learning researcher turned founder and investor. He runs Nazaré Ventures, an early-stage fund for AI infrastructure.

His 1997 Cambridge PhD was on mixtures of experts. That neural network design now sits inside many frontier models. At Sun Microsystems he built search and storage systems.

He co-founded RPX, which went public in 2011. In 2013 he helped reboot Pantera Capital as a crypto firm. He co-founded Orchid in 2017.

Mixtures of experts at Cambridge, 1992 to 1997

A mixture of experts is a set of small models with a gate. The gate decides which model handles each input.

Waterhouse wrote his PhD on this design in the Cambridge University Engineering Department, under Tony Robinson. His college was Jesus College. The thesis, finished in October 1997, tested the design against strong rivals and reported where the design lost. He received the Isaac Newton prize for his PhD research. Cambridge conferred the degree on May 9, 1998.

  • With David MacKay and Tony Robinson, he published a Bayesian method for training the tree-shaped form. A Bayesian method tracks how uncertain each learned value is. A 2012 survey credits the paper as the earliest variational treatment of the design. Variational methods turn that training into an optimization problem. The paper has 229 citations.
  • The thesis found that flat mixtures beat tree-shaped ones. Language models that use mixtures of experts today route in the flat form. That later work reached the result by a separate path.
  • He put the design inside ABBOT, the Cambridge speech recognizer. A boosted mixture cut word error from 17.5% to 14.2% on one test set.
  • He published his code in the DELVE benchmark archive. He also compiled a 196-entry bibliography of the field.

Ultimode, 1997 to 2000

After Cambridge he joined Ultimode Systems, a four-person research firm in Berkeley, California. His partners were Wray Buntine, Jon Oliver and Rohan Baxter. The firm took research contracts from NASA and built products from the results. Through Ultimode he worked as a contractor at NASA Ames.

Ultimode turned AutoClass, a NASA tool that sorts unlabeled records into groups, into a product named ACPro. With NASA Ames scientists, the team used ACPro to cluster 497 reference spectra for a Mars lander study. ACPro later shipped with MineSet, the data mining suite from Silicon Graphics.

With Cygnus, he and Oliver applied mixtures of experts to the GlucoWatch biographer, a wrist-worn glucose monitor. The algorithm turned the raw sensor signal into a glucose reading. The FDA approved the device on March 22, 2001, with the algorithm inside. Five US patents and a 1999 paper cover the method.

On July 27, 1999, the four partners filed a patent on text personalization. The method builds a profile from the documents a person opens. It then recommends matching documents by email. The filing predates Google's AdWords by 15 months and its content-matched ads by more than three years. Some 250 later patents cite the filing, Google's among them.

Search and storage at Sun Microsystems, 2000 to 2004

In 2000 he joined InfraSearch, a startup formed by Gene Kan and Yaroslav Faybishenko and backed by Marc Andreessen. InfraSearch sent each query to live sources and merged the answers. A crawler cannot index that kind of content. Sun Microsystems bought the company in March 2001.

At Sun he worked on search inside JXTA, the company's peer-to-peer platform. Six patents from that work carry his name. He described the method in Distributed Search in P2P Networks, written with Kan, Faybishenko and David Doolin.

He then founded and directed the Honeycomb storage group. Honeycomb stored each object as coded fragments across equal nodes, and rebuilt lost fragments from the rest. Customers could search objects by their own labels and run their own code on the storage nodes. Sun filed the core patents on June 30, 2004, about 20 months before Amazon launched S3. He is the first-named inventor on the most cited of them. The product shipped as the Sun StorageTek 5800, with Stanford as its first official beta customer. Later patents from Amazon, MongoDB and Hitachi cite the Honeycomb filings. He left Sun in 2004.

Intellectual property, 2004 to 2013

In 2004 he became an entrepreneur in residence at Foundation Capital. There he helped with the firm's investment in Powerset, the search company that Microsoft bought in 2008.

In 2005 he started Pebble Partners. The firm valued patents for Intellectual Ventures and brokered patents for selected clients. Pebble was the largest outside consultant that Intellectual Ventures used for patent valuation.

In 2008 he co-founded RPX with John Amster, Geoffrey Barker and Eran Zur, and served as chief technology officer. RPX buys patents that could be used against its members, then licenses them to every member. The company went public on Nasdaq on May 4, 2011, and raised about $160 million.

In mid-2012 he co-founded Koru Ventures with Zur and Erez Levy. Koru lent to technology companies against their patents. Fortress Investment Group bought Koru about six months later and built its patent finance group on the team. Waterhouse joined Fortress as a managing director.

Bitcoin and Pantera, 2013 to 2016

In February 2013, while at Fortress, he began to study bitcoin. Dan Morehead joined the effort in June. Morehead had founded Pantera Capital in 2003 as a macro hedge fund. In October 2013 the effort left Fortress and rebooted Pantera as a crypto firm. The new Pantera invested only in bitcoin, other digital currencies and the companies around them. Waterhouse gives this account in his 2016 review of the period.

The press caught up in stages. Fortune linked him to a Fortress bitcoin fund on December 31, 2013. On March 19, 2014, Fortune reported his move from Fortress to a Pantera partnership. That week Fortress, Benchmark and Ribbit Capital took stakes in Pantera's bitcoin fund.

He was a partner in the reboot and the firm's chief technology officer. By his account he invested $30 million across two venture funds. The first fund backed Bitstamp, Xapo, Circle, BitPagos and 21. Pantera joined Circle's first round in October 2013. The second backed Civic, Chain, Chronicled, Abra, Align Commerce, Filament and Zcoin.

He sat on the boards of Bitstamp, a bitcoin exchange, and ChangeTip, a tipping service. He often spoke for the firm in public. In 2015 he shared a TechCrunch panel with Vitalik Buterin, the founder of Ethereum. He left Pantera in July 2016.

Orchid, from 2017

Orchid returned him to technical work. He co-founded Orchid Labs in 2017 with Brian Fox, Jay Freeman, Gustav Simonsson and Stephen Bell. He served as chief executive. Fox wrote the GNU Bash shell. Freeman built Cydia, the software store for jailbroken iPhones.

Orchid built a market for network routing. A user bought bandwidth from independent providers and could chain several providers into one private path. No single provider saw the whole path.

The company came out of stealth in October 2017 with a first white paper. The final design is in Orchid: A Decentralized Network Routing Market, dated November 18, 2019. Waterhouse is a named author. The paper rests on two mechanisms.

  • Selection. Providers lock up tokens to list their nodes. The client picks nodes at random, weighted by the tokens each provider has locked.
  • Payment. The user pays with tickets that work like lottery tickets. Only winning tickets settle on the Ethereum blockchain. The method makes payment per packet practical.

Orchid raised $4.7 million on October 26, 2017. The investors included Andreessen Horowitz, Sequoia, Polychain Capital and Draper Fisher Jurvetson. An SEC filing on April 20, 2018 showed $36.1 million raised in a token sale. By November 2019 the total had passed $48 million.

The network, the app and the OXT token launched on December 16, 2019. Private Internet Access and four other providers supplied bandwidth from the first day. Orchid later added storage and Orchid GenAI, a market that sold access to more than 30 AI models. The same tickets paid for each model call.

Nazaré Ventures, from 2023

In 2020 he joined Fabric Ventures as chief technology officer and venture partner. Fabric invests in blockchain networks. He left before he started Nazaré Ventures.

He started Nazaré Ventures in late 2023. Nazaré is an early-stage fund for AI infrastructure, and he is its founder and general partner. The fund held its first close, the first round of investor commitments, in July 2024. It announced its launch on November 12, 2024. The fund backs open-source infrastructure that makes AI models cheaper to train and run. Its name comes from Nazaré, the Portuguese town known for record waves.

He describes the fund as a return to his roots in AI. He also writes Robot Wave, the fund's newsletter.
